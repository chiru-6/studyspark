# Cellular Respiration

Cellular respiration is the process cells use to convert the chemical energy stored in glucose into ATP. ATP, or adenosine triphosphate, is the main energy currency of the cell. Almost every active process in the cell, from muscle contraction to protein synthesis, depends on a steady supply of ATP.

Cellular respiration happens in three main stages: glycolysis, the Krebs cycle, and the electron transport chain. Glycolysis takes place in the cytoplasm and does not require oxygen. During glycolysis, one molecule of glucose is split into two molecules of pyruvate, producing a small net gain of two ATP and two NADH.

When oxygen is available, pyruvate moves into the mitochondria. The mitochondria are organelles often described as the powerhouse of the cell because most ATP is produced there. Inside the mitochondrial matrix, pyruvate is converted to acetyl-CoA, which then enters the Krebs cycle.

The Krebs cycle is a series of enzyme-driven reactions that releases carbon dioxide as a waste product. Each turn of the Krebs cycle produces NADH and FADH2, which carry high-energy electrons. These electron carriers are essential because they deliver electrons to the final stage of respiration.

The electron transport chain is a group of proteins embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. As electrons move along the electron transport chain, their energy is used to pump hydrogen ions across the membrane. This creates a concentration gradient. The enzyme ATP synthase uses the flow of hydrogen ions back across the membrane to produce large amounts of ATP. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor, and it combines with electrons and hydrogen ions to form water.

When oxygen is not available, cells can rely on fermentation instead. Fermentation regenerates NAD+ so that glycolysis can continue, but it produces far less ATP than aerobic respiration. In human muscle cells, fermentation produces lactic acid, while yeast cells produce ethanol and carbon dioxide.

Overall, aerobic respiration can produce roughly 30 to 32 ATP from a single glucose molecule. This efficiency is why organisms that use oxygen can support large, active bodies and complex nervous systems.
